The TFCC (triangular fibrocartilage complex) is a small "meniscus" of the wrist that stabilises the joint between the radius and the ulna, on the little-finger side. A tear, after a fall or through wear, causes pain on the inner (little-finger) side of the wrist, especially when turning the forearm. Treatment starts with rest and a splint; if the pain persists, wrist arthroscopy (keyhole surgery) makes it possible to clean up or repair the tear.

## What is the TFCC?

The TFCC is a structure made up of a disc of fibrocartilage and ligaments, located between the head of the ulna (the forearm bone on the little-finger side) and the carpal bones. It has two roles:

- **Cushioning** the pressure transmitted from the hand to the forearm, on the ulnar (little-finger) side of the wrist
- **Stabilising** the distal radioulnar joint, which allows you to turn the palm up or down (pronation-supination)

There are two main types of tear:

- **Traumatic tears**, after a fall on the hand, a sudden twist or an impact (racket, golf club). They are sometimes associated with a [wrist fracture](wrist-fracture.html).
- **Degenerative tears**, through gradual wear, often encouraged by an ulna that is slightly longer than the radius: this is then called [ulnocarpal impaction](ulnocarpal-impaction.html).

## What are the symptoms?

- Pain on the inner side of the wrist (little-finger side), brought on by turning movements: turning a key, using a screwdriver, carrying a saucepan, pushing up on the hand to get up
- A painful snap or clunk during certain movements
- Loss of strength for gripping or carrying
- A feeling of instability, sometimes an abnormal prominence of the head of the ulna on the back of the wrist

## How is it diagnosed?

The clinical examination looks for pain at a precise point on the ulnar side of the wrist and tests the stability of the distal radioulnar joint, comparing it with the other side.

- **X-rays**: they rule out a fracture and measure the length of the ulna relative to the radius.
- **CT arthrogram** (CT scan after injection of contrast dye into the joint): it shows the TFCC tear and any associated injuries of the ligaments or cartilage.

Arthroscopy remains the most precise test: it confirms the tear and allows it to be treated at the same time.

## What are the non-surgical treatments?

Very recent traumatic TFCC tears can sometimes be treated simply with a **custom-made thermoplastic splint** that blocks pronation-supination.

Partial tears can sometimes recover after well-conducted **rehabilitation**.

## When is surgery needed?

Surgery is considered when the pain persists after several months of properly conducted treatment for a partial tear, or straight away in the case of clear instability of the distal radioulnar joint with a complete tear. The choice of procedure depends on the location of the tear, how long ago it happened and any associated ulnocarpal impaction.

## How is the operation performed?

The operation is done by **wrist arthroscopy**: a small camera and fine instruments are inserted through incisions of a few millimetres. It is done as a day case, under regional anaesthesia (anaesthesia of the arm).

Depending on the tear:

- **Debridement**: a central tear, in an area with a poor blood supply that does not heal, is trimmed to remove the fragment that rubs.
- **Reattachment (suture)**: a tear at the edge, where the tissue has a good blood supply, is stitched back in place, by arthroscopy or through a short additional incision.
- **Associated procedure**: if the ulna is too long, an ulnar shortening can be performed at the same time.

## After the operation

- After a simple debridement, immobilisation is short and everyday activities are resumed within a few weeks.
- After a reattachment, the wrist and forearm are immobilised for 4 weeks to block turning movements and allow the repair to heal, then rehabilitation is prescribed.
- Return to sports that put strain on the wrist and to heavy manual work is gradual, usually after several months.

Complications are rare: stiffness, irritation of a small sensory nerve branch, complex regional pain syndrome. They are explained to you at your consultation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a TFCC tear heal on its own?
Yes, sometimes. Tears at the edge, which have a better blood supply, can heal with immobilisation, and many tears become only mildly bothersome with rest and rehabilitation.

### Does a TFCC tear seen on an MRI scan always need an operation?
No. Degenerative tears are common without any pain, especially after the age of 40. We treat a patient who has pain and whose examination matches the tear, not an isolated image.

### Will I be able to play tennis or golf again?
Most often, yes, after a gradual, supervised return. The timing depends on the procedure performed and is longer after a reattachment than after a debridement.

### Does wrist arthroscopy leave scars?
The incisions measure a few millimetres and usually become very discreet after a few months.
