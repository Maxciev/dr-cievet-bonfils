A finger or metacarpal fracture is a break in a phalanx (a finger bone) or a metacarpal (a long bone of the palm). It causes pain, swelling and sometimes a deformity or a finger that overlaps its neighbour when you make a fist. A stable fracture is treated with a splint; a displaced, rotated or joint fracture is most often fixed surgically.

## What is a finger or metacarpal fracture?

The hand has five metacarpals and fourteen phalanges: three in each finger and two in the thumb. These bones break from a direct blow (a punch, a crush injury, a ball), a fall or a twisting of the finger. A fracture of the neck of the fifth metacarpal after a punch is one of the most common ("boxer's fracture").

Several situations are distinguished, and they guide treatment:

- **undisplaced, stable fracture**: the fragments stay in place
- **displaced fracture**: the fragments are shifted, angled or shortened
- **rotated fracture**: the finger twists on itself and crosses over the neighbouring finger when bending
- **joint fracture**: the fracture line runs into a joint, with a risk of stiffness and osteoarthritis

## What are the symptoms?

- Sharp localised pain after the injury, sometimes with a crack felt
- Swelling (oedema) and bruising of the finger or the back of the hand
- Visible deformity, shortening or a knuckle that has lost its "bump" on the back of the hand
- A finger that overlaps its neighbour when you make a fist, a sign of rotation
- Difficulty bending or straightening the finger

## How is it diagnosed?

The clinical examination looks above all for a rotation problem: you are asked to close your fist gently to check that all the nails point to the same place. Front and side X-rays confirm the fracture and measure how far it is displaced. A CT scan is sometimes requested for complex joint fractures. If there is an associated wound, the fracture is called open and needs emergency treatment (see [hand emergencies](hand-emergencies.html)).

## What are the non-surgical treatments?

Undisplaced, stable fractures are treated without an operation:

- a **splint**, or a custom-made thermoplastic **splint**, leaving the uninvolved joints free, or **buddy strapping** (the injured finger is attached to its neighbour with strapping)
- immobilisation for a few weeks, usually 3 to 6 depending on the bone and the type of fracture
- frequent **follow-up X-rays** in the first weeks, to check that the fracture does not shift
- painkillers and elevation of the hand to limit swelling

Fingers do not tolerate prolonged immobilisation well: movement is resumed as soon as stability allows, often with the help of a hand therapist (physiotherapist).

## When is surgery needed?

Surgery is offered when the fracture is:

- **displaced** or angled beyond what the hand can tolerate
- **rotated** (overlapping finger), because rotation cannot be corrected with a splint
- **into the joint** with a step in the cartilage surface
- **unstable**, meaning it shifts again inside the splint
- **open** or associated with an injury to a tendon, nerve or blood vessel

Timing matters: a fracture that heals in a poor position (malunion) is harder to correct later. It is better to see a hand surgeon within the first few days.

## How is the operation performed?

The surgeon puts the fragments back in place (reduction), then holds them (internal fixation) with hardware suited to the fracture:

- **pins (K-wires)**: thin metal rods inserted through the skin, often without opening, removed after a few weeks
- **screws** alone, for some oblique or joint fractures
- a **plate and screws**, when the fixation needs to be strong enough to allow early movement

The operation is done as a day case, under regional anaesthesia of the arm.

## After the operation

- A protective splint is often worn for a few weeks, leaving free the joints that can be left free
- Rehabilitation starts early to limit stiffness, the main risk of hand fractures
- The pins (K-wires) are removed in the operating theatre under local anaesthesia, usually around the fourth to sixth week; screws and plates are most often left in place unless they cause discomfort
- Return to work and sport depends on the bone, the fixation and your activity

Possible complications are stiffness, malunion, delayed healing, infection or [complex regional pain syndrome](complex-regional-pain-syndrome.html). They are explained to you at the consultation.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a small deformity remain after a boxer's fracture?
Yes, a slight tilt of the neck of the fifth metacarpal is often well tolerated and can be treated without surgery; the loss of the knuckle's "bump" is then mainly cosmetic, but a rotation must always be corrected.

### How long does a broken finger take to heal?
The bone is usually strong enough for everyday use after four to six weeks, but complete healing takes at least 3 months; suppleness and strength continue to improve for several months.

### Is a plaster cast always needed?
No. Custom-made splints and buddy strapping have largely replaced plaster casts for fingers: they immobilise only what is necessary and limit stiffness.

### Why is my finger still swollen and stiff after the fracture has healed?
Swelling often persists for several weeks, or even a few months; rehabilitation, regular exercises and sometimes a corrective (positioning) splint help to recover movement.
